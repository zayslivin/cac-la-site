Step by step

01

A sealed, fluid-filled joint

Your knuckle is a synovial joint: two bones, each capped with slick cartilage, sealed inside a capsule of synovial fluid. That fluid lubricates the joint — and holds dissolved gases, mostly carbon dioxide, the same way a sealed soda holds its fizz.

02

You pull the joint apart

Bending or tugging the finger stretches the capsule and pulls the bone surfaces apart. The sealed space inside gets bigger, so the pressure of the fluid drops sharply — there’s suddenly more room than fluid to fill it.

03

A gas cavity snaps into being — the pop

Once pressure falls far enough, dissolved gas can no longer stay in solution. It rushes out all at once to form a gas-filled cavity. A 2015 MRI study (“Real-Time Visualization of Joint Cavitation”) showed the crack coincides with this cavity forming, not a bubble bursting. That rapid formation is the sound you hear.

04

The reset

The cavity lingers as the gas slowly redissolves back into the fluid over roughly 20 minutes. Until it does, the joint physically can’t crack again — which is exactly why you have to wait before that knuckle will pop a second time.

Myth

“It causes arthritis.” Long-term studies — including one researcher who cracked the knuckles of only one hand for 60 years — found no link between knuckle-cracking and arthritis.

True

The pop is the cavity forming. Not bones grinding, not a bubble collapsing. It’s dissolved gas flashing into a cavity as joint pressure drops — a process called tribonucleation.
